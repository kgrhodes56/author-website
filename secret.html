Ravenloft: The Rising

Book Two

Prologue

The torches burned low in the old house, their flames bending away from the circle carved into the floor. Iron and ash thickened the air. Outside, Winter Creek held its breath, the way a town does when it knows something unforgivable is about to happen.

High in the rafters, a raven watched. Its blue eyes reflected the sigils below.

The circle was carved, not drawn. Deep grooves filled with iron dust and ash formed a lattice of old magic. Blood channels branched outward like veins waiting to carry the sacrifice into the lock. Four stones sat at the cardinal points. The northern stone hummed with a low, mournful tone. The eastern stone rang sharp as a blade. The southern stone throbbed like a heartbeat. The western stone whispered like wind through bones.

Thirteen cloaked witches stood around the circle. Hooded. Silent. Their resolve held, but their hands betrayed them.

At the center knelt the Architect.

Human, then too human. His obsidian eyes tracked each witch with a predator's patience. The Mirror Bowl beside him rippled, reflecting not his face but the monstrous shape beneath it. A single raven feather lay at the northern anchor, placed there by a tradition older than the house itself.

The eldest witch scattered iron filings along the outer ring. The second poured ash from the First Fire. The third crushed bone powder between trembling fingers. Each ingredient hissed as it touched the circle, as if the ritual recognized its own birthright.

The Architect smiled. "Thirteen witches who claim to guard the world, yet you tremble like children."

The youngest witch flinched. "We should not be doing this. He built the lock. He knows every flaw."

"That is why it will hold," the second insisted, though her voice wavered.

"And why it will break," the Architect murmured.

The eldest ignored him and lifted the Binding Blade. The metal was forged from the same ore as the original lock. The room seemed to recoil from its cold sheen.

"You think age gives you wisdom," the Architect said. "It only gives you more to lose."

The sigils ignited one by one. Frost crawled across the windows. The house groaned, its beams bowing inward as if bracing for what was coming.

The Architect leaned forward. "Iron. Ash. Bone. Blood. You cling to old tools because you have no new ideas."

But the eldest witch's hand hovered over the final sigil. This one was not his. This one was older than the Architect. Older than the house. Older than the thirteen.

The raven croaked once, a low and rattling warning.

The Architect's smile faltered. "What is that?"

The eldest pressed her palm to the sigil.

It pulsed once. White, then black, then something colorless and impossible.

The Architect recoiled. "No. That mark is not mine."

"We added what you could not," the eldest said.

"You tampered with my design," he hissed. "You fools. You do not understand what you have invoked."

"We do not need to understand it," the third witch said. "We only need it to hold you."

The circle roared to life. Shadows clawed at the air as the Architect surged against his bindings.

"One breath," he whispered. "One mistake. That is all I need."

The youngest witch hesitated. The Architect's shadow stretched toward the edge of the sigils.

The raven dove from the rafters. Its wings cracked the air. The youngest startled back into position.

The circle stabilized.

The eldest did not hesitate.

The blade descended.

Blood struck the sigils. Light flared. The Architect's form split into shadow and teeth as the lock dragged him downward into the seam between worlds. The house screamed.

As he fell, he reached.

His hand, no longer flesh, brushed the youngest witch's wrist.

Cold. Then burning.

The mark bloomed beneath the skin. Lines of light crawled up the witch's arm like molten veins. The sigils answered, flaring in the same pattern.

The Architect's voice curled through the collapsing lock. "There you are. The one who trembles. The one who breaks."

The mark pulsed once. A heartbeat that did not belong to the witch.

"I choose you. I choose your blood. I choose your future."

The youngest screamed as the light carved itself into bone.

"This is not a wound," the Architect whispered. "This is a door."

The circle collapsed inward. His final words echoed through the room.

"Seal me. Bury me. Hide me beneath your floorboards. I will rise through your children."

Silence fell.

The youngest collapsed, clutching their wrist where the mark still burned. The others did not notice. They were too busy sealing the floor, burying the ritual, burying the truth.

Above them, the house settled. The lock held. The Architect slept.

But the raven remained.

It watched the youngest witch tremble. It watched the mark pulse beneath their skin. Then, with a single beat of its wings, it vanished into the rafters.

The mark remained.

And the bloodline it touched would one day return to Winter Creek.

Chapter One

Rowan read the letter for the fourth time that morning, though she already knew every word by heart.

Dear Dr. Ashford,

We are so pleased to offer you the position of Chief of Emergency Medicine here at Winter Creek General Hospital. Our community has been hoping for someone with your experience and heart, and we believe you'll be a wonderful fit for both the hospital and the town.

If you choose to accept, please reach out to Dr. Amy Whitmore so she can help you get settled. We've already arranged a small cottage for you near the hospital. Nothing fancy, but cozy, quiet, and close to everything you'll need.

Your salary and benefits are outlined in the enclosed packet, and Dr. Whitmore will answer any questions when you arrive. We're a small place, but we take good care of our own.

Welcome to Winter Creek, Dr. Ashford. We're looking forward to meeting you.

Warmly, Winter Creek General Administration

She lowered the paper slowly.

She should have felt relieved. It was a new job, a clean slate, and a chance to outrun the strange restlessness that had been gnawing at her for months.

The moment she touched the parchment, the tug in her chest tightened again. Sharper this time. Almost painful. The memory returned without warning: the fluorescent lights buzzing above the emergency room, the smell of antiseptic and burnt coffee, and the hollow tone of a monitor flatlining behind a curtain she had not reached fast enough. A teenage boy lay motionless on the bed. Rowan called for the defibrillator, reached for the paddles, and confirmed the setting with the nurse. Then something surged down her arms and flooded her hands. Not electricity. Not adrenaline. Something else.

Heat and light flooded her palms as she pressed the paddles to the boy's chest. The unit had been set to 120 joules, but the output registered 360. Three times the intended charge. The shock was too much for his already failing heart. Rowan checked the setting herself. The nurse had entered it correctly. No one could explain the surge. Afterward, people talked. They pointed. They blamed.

The review cleared Rowan and blamed equipment failure, a freak accident no one could have predicted. She signed the final report with a hand that refused to stay steady, then pressed her palm flat against the desk until the shaking stopped. The explanation never felt right. She remembered the surge, the heat, and the way something inside her slipped loose for one terrible second. She did not understand it. She had spent months refusing to try.

Now, with the letter in her hands, the same sensation flickered beneath her skin. Faint. Unsteady. Terrifying. Winter Creek General was her chance to begin again and leave the trauma of that day behind.

The dreams had started the same night the letter arrived.

Always the same.

A dark room. A heartbeat that wasn't hers. A voice whispering a name she didn't know.

Ezra.

At first she blamed stress. Then exhaustion. Then the strange static that sometimes gathered beneath her skin when she had gone too long without rest.

But the dreams didn't stop.

They sharpened.

And the tug in her chest grew stronger.

By the time she packed her car and started the long drive to Winter Creek, her tattoos flickered faintly beneath her sleeves, constellations shifting like they were trying to orient themselves toward something.

Toward someone.

She crossed the county line just after dusk. The sky was too clear, the moon too sharp, the silence too absolute. The kind of silence that felt aware.

The moment her tires crossed the Winter Creek town line, the world changed.

The air thickened. The sky sharpened. Her magic surged so suddenly she gasped, gripping the steering wheel as warmth flooded her chest.

The tug snapped tight.

A thread pulled taut.

A voice whispered through her mind, soft and familiar in a way that made no sense.

Ezra.

Rowan slammed the brakes. Gravel skidded beneath her tires. She stumbled out of the car and onto the cold grass at the roadside, breath shaking. The frigid air bit at her cheeks, sharp with pine and the promise of rain. "Tachycardia. Just a panic attack," she murmured, pressing two fingers to her racing pulse as if she could steady it by will alone.

The night wrapped around her like a shiver. The silence was absolute, too heavy, too expectant.

A rustle drew her gaze upward.

A raven perched on the road sign, feathers gleaming like spilled ink, blue eyes fixed on her with unsettling intelligence. It did not move. It simply watched.

The world tilted.

Her knees buckled.

Magic surged through her warm, grounding, like someone had reached into her chest and steadied her heartbeat with their own. Her tattoos flared beneath her skin, constellations waking one by one.

A faint golden sigil bloomed on her palm, lines shifting like molten metal.

It pulsed once.

Twice.

Syncing with a heartbeat that was not hers.

Far away, in a house she had never seen, Ezra Hawthorne jerked awake, breath ragged, shadows bending toward him as if listening.

He felt the pull. He felt the thread. He felt her.

Rowan clutched her glowing palm. "Who are you?"

The wind did not answer.

But the magic did.

A string tightened. A connection snapped into place. Something ancient stirred beneath Winter Creek.

The raven lifted its wings, feathers bristling as if it felt the awakening too.

Rowan knew, with a certainty that terrified her, that whatever had been calling her for months had finally found her.

The house has awakened.

Book Two is rising.
